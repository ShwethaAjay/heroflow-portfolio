01 · Context

A studio at a crossroads.

Lemonlight is a traditional video production company with an elite client list: Delta, Apple, Walmart, Amazon. But the industry was shifting faster than their workflow could keep up.

Studios that stopped at post-production were losing relevance. The demand had expanded into content repurposing, social-native formats, and data-driven editing. Capabilities that required either a much larger team, or a smarter system.

AI was the obvious answer. But the question was never whether to adopt it. It was how. Generic AI video generation wasn't going to fly with a client list of this caliber. Enterprise brands don't want synthetic footage when they already own pristine, brand-accurate B-roll.

The real opportunity wasn't to replace their footage with AI. It was to make their existing footage infinitely more accessible and usable.

Problem Statement · IYA Format

A video production manager who feels overwhelmed by the volume of unused campaign footage needs to quickly locate, contextualize, and repurpose the right clips but has no searchable system, forcing costly manual reviews and missed content windows.

02 · Problem Space

The brief was simple. The stakes weren't.

Five days. One client. A working prototype that had to prove technical feasibility to a company with Delta, Apple, and Walmart on its roster. The margin for a vague, concept-only pitch was zero.

The brief asked us to find a "suitable means" for Lemonlight to incorporate AI into their workflow. That openness was its own trap. Without a clear constraint, every direction felt equally valid and equally risky. We could spend two days pursuing a direction that engineering couldn't build, or design a technically sound product that solved the wrong problem entirely.

On top of that, the AI video space was noisy. Every "solution" we looked at was either generating footage that wouldn't pass enterprise brand standards, or adding yet another tool to a workflow that was already too fragmented.

03 · Iteration

Three dead ends before the right door.

Design sprints don't move in a straight line. Each bad idea was load-bearing. Without the failures, we never would have found TwelveLabs.

Failed Attempt

V1: Full AI Generation Pipeline

We proposed letting AI generate new video clips to fill content gaps. Engineering quickly flagged this as out-of-scope for a 5-day build, and the client rightfully noted that synthetic video doesn't meet brand standards for Delta or Apple.

Key Insight

The "Delta Dawn" Moment

Researching cultural trends, we found an organic spike in the "Delta Dawn" trend. This reframed the entire product: the value isn't generating content — it's responding to moments using owned footage you can actually find.

Failed Attempt

V2: Manual Tagging Interface

We designed a manual tagging system for editors. Stakeholder feedback on Day 2 was blunt: editors are already overwhelmed. Adding a tagging workflow wouldn't be adopted. We scrapped it overnight.

Pivot

TwelveLabs as the Backbone

Involving engineering early surfaced TwelveLabs as the video analysis layer. Their API could semantically understand video content and generate searchable text descriptions automatically. No manual tagging required. This unlocked the whole product.

Stakeholder Feedback

Editors Want to Describe, Not Search

User testing (even scrappy hallway testing) revealed that editors think in narrative terms — "warm, vibrant, airplane taking off" — not file names. The UX had to be natural language-first, not filter-first.

Pivot

LLM as the Edit Orchestrator

Instead of a traditional editor interface, we designed a prompt-to-edit flow: describe what you want → LLM interprets intent + context (brand data, pitch deck tone) → retrieves and sequences assets. Simpler. More powerful.

What actually kept the sprint from falling apart:

01

Engineering in the room during ideation

Not for sign-off. For shaping. When we whiteboarded the TwelveLabs integration, our engineer immediately knew the API's indexing latency would affect the UX flow. That conversation happened on Day 3, not after mockups were finalized.

02

One stakeholder check-in that changed the product

Mid-sprint, we showed Lemonlight a rough walk-through of the tagging UI. Their feedback: editors already have 12 tools open. We had been designing for power users. We needed to design for people who are tired. The whole interaction model shifted from control to conversation.

03

Deciding what HeroFlow was not

We cut the analytics dashboard, multi-user collaboration, and a client-facing export portal. Not because they were bad ideas. Because including them meant none of them would work. The prompt-to-edit flow is the only thing we built, and it's the only thing that needed to exist for the pitch to land.

04 · Validation

Day 5. We weren't sure we'd make it.

The night before the final pitch, the prototype's natural language search was returning inconsistent results. Engineering stayed up fixing the TwelveLabs integration. Design rebuilt the UI flow to account for the latency. We presented on 40 minutes of sleep with a live working demo.

05 · Impact

What the sprint unlocked after.

The prototype revealed something we hadn't fully designed for: the prompt-to-edit flow changed how Lemonlight's team thought about their own footage archive.

During the live demo, one of their producers typed a natural language query and paused when the result surfaced. Not because it was wrong. Because they had forgotten that clip existed. They had produced it 18 months earlier for a Delta campaign, and it had been sitting in cold storage ever since.

That moment reframed HeroFlow from an efficiency tool into something closer to a creative memory system. It wasn't just about speed. It was about making high-production footage usable across time, not just once at the point of delivery.

01

A new internal workflow proposal

After the pitch, Lemonlight's team started discussing how HeroFlow could integrate with their existing post-production handoff process. The sprint produced a design artifact that sparked a real operational conversation.

02

Brand context as a product feature

The HeroFacts layer, where the LLM ingests pitch decks and brand guidelines to contextualize searches, became the detail that differentiated HeroFlow from any off-the-shelf video search tool. It wasn't in the original brief. It emerged from watching how their team actually talked about footage.

06 · Solution

What we built.

HeroFlow is a smart content repurposing platform that connects a brand's owned video library to an AI-powered editing interface, all inside the Lemonlight client portal.

The core loop: production footage gets ingested, TwelveLabs analyzes and generates semantic text descriptions, an LLM integrates brand context (pitch decks, HeroFacts, tone guides), the client types what they need in plain English, and the system retrieves and sequences the right assets.

No synthetic video. No manual tagging. No editor required.

Outcome

The solution has been receiving mostly positive feedback. Clients responded to the premise immediately: the idea that their owned footage could become a searchable, living asset library rather than a hard drive collecting dust.

We're planning our first iteration cycle based on early feedback: improving the natural language parsing for non-English descriptions, adding timeline-based clip trimming directly in the UI, and exploring a lighter onboarding path for smaller clients who aren't working within an existing Lemonlight project.

The prompt-to-edit flow wasn't in the original brief. It emerged from watching editors work. The best features in this sprint came from observing behavior, not solving the stated problem. Lemonlight didn't ask for a natural language interface. They asked us to find a suitable means to use AI. We found it by watching what made their producers stop and think.

We didn't build a tool. We built a new relationship between a brand and its own footage.
